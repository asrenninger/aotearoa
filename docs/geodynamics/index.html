The made land
deep time — c. 1400 CE
Chapter One

Out of Gondwana,
and the continent that drowned

New Zealand is the high ground of Zealandia, a continent the size of the Indian subcontinent that lies almost entirely beneath the sea.

Around 85 million years ago, a slab of continental crust began to tear away from the eastern edge of Gondwana. As the Tasman Sea opened behind it, the fragment stretched, thinned, and slowly sank. Geologists call it Zealandia, or Te Riu-a-Māui — a continent of roughly five million square kilometres, of which about ninety-four per cent now lies underwater.

For tens of millions of years there was barely any land at all. By the late Oligocene, perhaps twenty-five million years ago, Zealandia had drowned so completely that some scientists argue almost nothing stood above the waves. The New Zealand we know is young: most of its mountains have been pushed up only in the last five million years, as the plate boundary running through it began to crunch.

Chapter Two

The Ring of Fire,
and the country built on its seam

The Pacific is ringed by the planet's most violent geology. New Zealand sits directly on the boundary where the Pacific and Australian plates grind past and beneath one another.

Aotearoa sits near the heart of the Ring of Fire.

Te Ahi Kā o Te Moana-nui-a-Kiwa · The Pacific Ring of Fire
Indicative volcanoes and simplified plate boundary
Step 1 · The rim

A horseshoe of volcanoes and trenches wraps the Pacific Ocean, from the Andes to Alaska to Japan to New Zealand. Around three-quarters of the world's active volcanoes, and most of its largest earthquakes, occur along this rim.

Step 2 · The seam
2 plates
Pacific and Australian, meeting through New Zealand

The boundary runs the length of the country. Off the North Island's east coast, the Pacific plate dives beneath the Australian along the Hikurangi subduction zone, which in the worst case could produce a magnitude 9 earthquake and tsunami.

Step 3 · The Alpine Fault
1717
The fault's last major rupture

Down the spine of the South Island runs the Alpine Fault, moving the land past itself at around 27 millimetres a year. It ruptures about every 250 years; scientists put the chance of the next one within fifty years at about 75 per cent.

Step 4 · The Taupō Volcanic Zone

In the central North Island, the same forces open a rift of volcanoes — Ruapehu, Tongariro, Tarawera, Whakaari, and the vast caldera of Taupō.

Taupō's Ōruanui eruption, around 26,000 years ago, was the largest on Earth in the last 70,000 years.

Chapter Three

A new island,
and the people who watched it rise

Rangitoto, the youngest of Auckland's volcanoes, rose from the sea between about 1400 and 1500 CE, and people were there to see it.

Auckland is home to fifty-three volcanoes. The Auckland Volcanic Field has erupted, somewhere, dozens of times across the last 200,000 years or so. Most of its cones erupted once and went quiet. Then, between about 1400 and 1500 CE, a new vent opened in the Hauraki Gulf and built the largest of them all: Rangitoto, the broad shield island that frames the city's harbour today.

Source: GNS Science; Te Ara; Science Learning Hub

We know people watched, because they left their feet behind. On neighbouring Motutapu Island, human and dog footprints are preserved between layers of Rangitoto's ash — pressed into the ground, then sealed by the next fall. Māori stood on that shore and saw a mountain born.

No detailed account of the eruption is recorded. The footprints show that people were on Motutapu while Rangitoto was erupting.

Act II · A Witnessed Land
Fire in living memory
c. 1250 — 1931
Chapter Four

The descendants arrive,
and the land remembers fire

Māori settlement coincided with one of the country's largest eruptions in a thousand years. The volatile land entered the oral record as gods, ancestors and a map of fire.

When East Polynesian voyagers settled Aotearoa around 1250–1300 CE, they found a land that was still volcanically active. Within decades of first settlement, around 1314, Mount Tarawera erupted in the Kaharoa event, the largest eruption in New Zealand for some seven hundred years and several times the size of the one that would make Tarawera famous in 1886.

That volatility is woven through Māori tradition. Earthquakes and eruptions are the work of Rūaumoko, the unborn child of the earth mother Papatūānuku, carried in the underworld and given fire to keep warm; his stirrings shake the ground.

A legend that maps a rift

THE TRADITION'S FIRE-ROUTE, ON THE REAL VOLCANOES IT NAMES

In the founding tradition of Ngāti Tūwharetoa, Ngātoroirangi, a navigator and priest, climbed Tongariro and was trapped near its summit by a freezing southerly. Dying of cold, he called to his sisters, Kuiwai and Haungaroa, far to the north on Whakaari, to send him fire from the ancestral homeland of Hawaiki. It came as two fire spirits, Te Pupu and Te Hoata, who carried the fire underground from Whakaari and broke the surface again and again on the way south: at Rotorua, Tarawera, the boiling ground around Taupō, and at last Tongariro. Every place they surfaced is a real geothermal or volcanic centre, and in sequence they trace, almost exactly, the line of the Taupō Volcanic Zone. The tradition describes a single vein of underground fire running from the sea to the mountains.

Traditions that carry a real geological event down the generations are studied as geomythology. Across the Tasman, Aboriginal Australian stories appear to preserve the shape of coastlines the sea drowned more than seven thousand years ago. The route of Ngātoroirangi's fire follows a rift that no one on the ground could have seen whole.

Chapter Five

The newcomers read the weather,
and clear the forest

Europeans arrived expecting a Britain of the South. They found a wetter, windier, more violent country and set about remaking its surface.

To the British settlers of the nineteenth century, New Zealand's climate was a selling point: temperate, green, familiar enough to grow English grass and graze English sheep. The reality was less obliging: a narrow, mountainous country in the path of the Roaring Forties, where the weather changes by the hour and the rivers flood without warning.

Their largest mark on the climate was indirect. To make pasture, they burned and felled the forest at a pace that dwarfed what had come before. Forest that had covered most of the country before people arrived — already reduced by earlier Māori burning — was cut back to less than a third of the land.

It was the first deliberate, mass reshaping of the New Zealand environment by its newest arrivals.

Chapter Six

Tarawera, 1886,
and the night the terraces died

The country's first great tourist wonder, the Pink and White Terraces, vanished in a single night.

In the early hours of 10 June 1886, Mount Tarawera split open along a rift many kilometres long and erupted. Ash buried the surrounding villages; about 120 people died, most of them Māori. The Pink and White Terraces — Te Otukapuarangi and Te Tarata, silica staircases that drew visitors from across the world — were destroyed. They were perhaps the largest deposits of silica sinter, or geyserite, anywhere on Earth, built up over centuries from the mineral-laden water of the geothermal springs and geysers below.

Eleven days before, a boatload of tourists guided by Sophia Hinerangi saw a waka taua, a war canoe crowded with figures, glide through the mist on the lake and vanish. It matched no canoe known on the water. An aged Māori priest, the tohunga Tūhoto Ariki, read it as a warning: the people would be punished for selling the sacred terraces to sightseers. Whether it was a mirage, or a swell thrown up by the mountain already stirring, has been argued ever since. When Tarawera erupted, Tūhoto, reputedly over a hundred years old, was buried in his house and later dug out alive.

A wonder that had taken centuries to deposit was gone in hours.

Chapter Seven

The settler century shakes,
and rebuilds in Art Deco

Twice in the colonial era the ground reshaped a city: in 1855 it lifted the shore of the future capital out of the sea, and in 1931 it flattened Napier, which was then rebuilt whole.

In 1855, a magnitude 8.2 earthquake on the Wairarapa fault, the largest in New Zealand's recorded history, heaved the land around Wellington upward by metres. It lifted the harbour edge so far that a strip of today's central city, later extended by reclamation, rose out of the water.

On 3 February 1931, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck Hawke's Bay; GeoNet's preferred moment magnitude for it is 7.4. It killed 256 people, the country's deadliest natural disaster, razed central Napier and wrecked Hastings. The seabed off Napier rose by around two metres, draining a lagoon and adding land the city still uses. Napier rebuilt fast, in the clean lines of the moment, and became one of the world's great Art Deco towns.

Act III · The Changed Air
An invisible reshaping
c. 1850 — 2000
Chapter Eight

The Anthropocene arrives,
and the air starts to change

For most of human history the air contained about 280 parts of carbon dioxide per million. In a century and a half, emissions have pushed it past 420, and New Zealand has measured the change from its own clean corner of the planet.

Bubbles of ancient air, trapped in Antarctic ice, show carbon dioxide holding steady for thousands of years — around 280 parts per million through the whole rise of human civilisation.

280 ppm
The pre-industrial baseline

In 1958, a young scientist named Charles Keeling began measuring CO₂ on a Hawaiian volcano. His record, the Keeling Curve, shows the concentration climbing year after year.

In 1972, New Zealand opened its own station at Baring Head, on the wild coast south of Wellington, sampling air that has crossed the Southern Ocean. It is the longest continuous CO₂ record in the Southern Hemisphere.

1972
Baring Head record begins, at 326 ppm

Both records now read above 420 parts per million, a level the planet has not seen in millions of years.

Carbon was not the only thing people put into the air. Another family of gases, from the world's refrigerators and spray cans, would by the 1980s open a hole in the ozone layer over Antarctica.

Chapter Nine

The hole in the sky,
and why the south paid most

A class of miracle chemicals tore a hole in the ozone layer over Antarctica. New Zealand, Australia and the southern tip of South America sat closest to the damage.

In 1974, two chemists warned that chlorofluorocarbons — CFCs, the inert gases prized in fridges, aerosols and foam — would drift to the stratosphere and destroy ozone. In 1985, British Antarctic Survey scientists reported something worse than predicted: each austral spring, a hole was opening in the ozone layer over Antarctica.

When measurements began, the hole was small. Through the 1980s it opened wider almost every spring as chlorine from CFCs built up in the stratosphere over the cold Antarctic vortex.

In 1987, the world signed the Montreal Protocol and began phasing out CFCs. It is widely called the most successful environmental treaty ever agreed. But the chemicals already aloft would keep destroying ozone for decades.

1987
Montreal Protocol signed

The hole stayed near its peak through the 1990s and early 2000s. On its largest single day, 9 September 2000, it covered nearly 30 million square kilometres, larger than the whole of North America and far larger than Antarctica itself. New Zealand and Australia sit just north of its edge: the hole rarely covers them, but ozone-poor air spills north over the Southern Ocean towards them each spring.

29.9M km²
Record single-day extent, 9 Sep 2000

Since the mid-2000s the hole has stopped growing and slowly begun to recover. Full recovery over Antarctica is projected for around 2066.

New Zealand already had naturally thin ozone and exceptionally clear air; peak summer UV runs around forty per cent higher than at comparable latitudes in the north. Its melanoma rate, like Australia's, is among the highest in the world, about twice the rates in the United Kingdom, the United States and Canada. The ozone hole added to that risk; it did not create it.

The first replacements, HCFCs, still thinned the ozone a little. Their successors, HFCs, spare the ozone but warm the planet, and are now being phased down under the 2016 Kigali Amendment to the same treaty.

Chapter Ten

Still shaking,
and learning to live with it

The deep geology never went quiet. In the last fifteen years earthquakes have destroyed central Christchurch and lifted parts of the Kaikōura coast by metres.

On 4 September 2010, a magnitude 7.1 earthquake struck near Christchurch in the early morning. No one died. Then, on 22 February 2011, a shallow magnitude 6.2 aftershock hit directly beneath the city at lunchtime. It killed 185 people, collapsed buildings, and turned whole suburbs to liquefied silt.

Depth and distance from a city matter as much as magnitude. The shallow 6.2 beneath Christchurch was deadlier than the larger Kaikōura earthquake of 2016, which ruptured more than twenty faults at once, a world record, and killed two. New Zealand now builds, insures and plans for ground that will move again.

People in a national panel survey are asked how often, in the past month, they worried that a big earthquake might hit their region. In 2018–19, about — of Canterbury respondents said at least some of the time, against — of respondents elsewhere in the country. Canterbury's share has been roughly twice the national rate, and close to three times in the early waves. Its gap with the rest of the country has roughly halved since the question was first asked in 2012–13. Chapter Eleven

The heat arrives,
and the summers lengthen

New Zealand warmed by 1.36°C between 1909 and 2025. The change is most visible in the summers, on land and in the surrounding ocean.

New Zealand's longest temperature record, the seven-station series, shows the country warming by 1.36°C between 1909 and 2025. The reanalysis record below begins in 1940 and shows the same rise; its warmest years cluster in the last decade.

The warming is not only on land. In the summer of 2017–18, a marine heatwave settled over the Tasman Sea, with surface waters 2–4°C above normal and up to 6–7°C in places; it was the country's hottest summer on record. Such marine heatwaves are becoming more frequent and more intense, reshaping fisheries and coastlines.

Heat arrives as longer summers, warmer nights and warmer seas.

Chapter Twelve

The climates ahead,
and the choice that shapes them

How much hotter New Zealand becomes is not yet decided. The projections fan out from the present according to how much carbon the world keeps adding.

The latest projections show the country's path splitting on the choices of this century. Under deep cuts, warming levels off below a degree above the 1995–2014 average, not far above today's climate. Under high emissions it keeps climbing, to 3.0–3.7°C above that average by 2090, with longer droughts in the north and east, heavier downpours and a rising sea.

Some sea-level rise is already locked in. Current national guidance asks councils to test a range of scenarios, roughly 0.5 to 0.9 metres of rise by 2090 against 1995–2014 before vertical land movement is added, with more after 2090. Where the land is sinking, as in parts of Wellington, subsidence adds 0.1 to 0.3 metres. New Zealand's cities sit on the coast, and parts of Napier and central Wellington stand on land raised by the 1931 and 1855 earthquakes.

Chapter Thirteen

The made land,
still being made

A country assembled by forces beyond any human hand is now being reshaped, at the scale of its climate, by one.

Aotearoa is the visible fraction of a drowned continent, raised on a plate boundary, studded with volcanoes, shaken on a schedule no one can quite predict. Fire and water built it, and still do.
