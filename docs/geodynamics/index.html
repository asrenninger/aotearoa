Act I · Fire & Stone
The made land
deep time — c. 1400 CE
Chapter One

Out of Gondwana,
and the continent that drowned

New Zealand is the high ground of Zealandia — a continent the size of the Indian subcontinent, almost entirely beneath the sea.

Around 85 million years ago, a slab of continental crust began to tear away from the eastern edge of Gondwana. As the Tasman Sea opened behind it, the fragment stretched, thinned, and slowly sank. Geologists call it Zealandia, or Te Riu-a-Māui — a continent of roughly five million square kilometres, of which about ninety-four per cent now lies underwater.

For tens of millions of years there was barely any land at all. By the late Oligocene, perhaps twenty-five million years ago, Zealandia had drowned so completely that some scientists argue almost nothing stood above the waves. The New Zealand we know is young: most of its mountains have been pushed up only in the last five million years, as the plate boundary running through it began to crunch.

A continent of one's own

To stand in New Zealand, then, is to stand on the few peaks of a sunken world — the part of Zealandia that the collision of two plates has shoved back into the air. That collision is the engine of everything that follows.

Chapter Two

The Ring of Fire,
and the country built on its seam

The Pacific is ringed by the planet's most violent geology. New Zealand sits directly on the boundary where the Pacific and Australian plates grind past and beneath one another.

Aotearoa sits near the heart of the Ring of Fire.

Step 1 · The rim

A horseshoe of volcanoes and trenches wraps the Pacific Ocean, from the Andes to Alaska to Japan to New Zealand. Around three-quarters of the world's active volcanoes — and most of its largest earthquakes — happen along this rim.

Step 2 · The seam
2 plates
Pacific and Australian, meeting through New Zealand

The boundary runs the length of the country. Off the North Island's east coast, the Pacific plate dives beneath the Australian along the Hikurangi subduction zone — capable, in the worst case, of a magnitude-9 earthquake and tsunami.

Step 3 · The Alpine Fault
1717
The fault's last major rupture

Down the spine of the South Island runs the Alpine Fault, moving the land past itself at around 27 millimetres a year. It ruptures roughly every three centuries; scientists put the chance of the next one within fifty years at about 75 per cent.

Step 4 · The Taupō Volcanic Zone

In the central North Island, the same forces open a rift of volcanoes — Ruapehu, Tongariro, Tarawera, Whakaari, and the vast caldera of Taupō.

Taupō's Ōruanui eruption, around 26,000 years ago, was the largest on Earth in the last 70,000 years.

Chapter Three

A new island,
and the people who watched it rise

Rangitoto, the youngest of Auckland's volcanoes, rose from the sea only about six centuries ago — and people were there to see it.

Auckland is home to fifty-three volcanoes. The Auckland Volcanic Field has erupted, somewhere, dozens of times across the last 190,000 years. Most of its cones erupted once and went quiet. Then, around 1400 CE, a new vent opened in the Hauraki Gulf and built the largest of them all: Rangitoto, the broad shield island that frames the city's harbour today.

Source: GNS Science; Te Ara; Science Learning Hub

We know people watched, because they left their feet behind. On neighbouring Motutapu Island, human and dog footprints are preserved between layers of Rangitoto's ash — pressed into the ground, then sealed by the next fall. Māori stood on that shore and saw a mountain born.

No story of the eruption itself survives in oral tradition. But the footprints are unarguable: humans arrived while the land was still making itself.

Act II · A Witnessed Land
Fire in living memory
c. 1250 — 1931
Chapter Four

The descendants arrive,
and the land remembers fire

Māori settlement coincided with one of the country's largest eruptions in a thousand years. The volatile land entered the oral record — as gods, ancestors, and a map of fire.

When East Polynesian voyagers settled Aotearoa around 1250–1300 CE, they arrived in a landscape that would not hold still. Within a few generations, around 1314, Mount Tarawera erupted in the Kaharoa event — the largest eruption in New Zealand for some seven hundred years, several times the size of the one that would make Tarawera famous in 1886.

That volatility is woven through Māori tradition. Earthquakes and eruptions are the work of Rūaumoko, the unborn child of the earth mother Papatūānuku, carried in the underworld and given fire to keep warm; his stirrings shake the ground.

A legend that maps a rift

THE TRADITION'S FIRE-ROUTE, ON THE REAL VOLCANOES IT NAMES

In the founding tradition of Ngāti Tūwharetoa, Ngātoroirangi, a navigator and priest, climbed Tongariro and was trapped near its summit by a freezing southerly. Dying of cold, he called to his sisters, Kuiwai and Haungaroa, far to the north on Whakaari, to send him fire from the ancestral homeland of Hawaiki. It came as two fire spirits, Te Pupu and Te Hoata, who carried the fire underground from Whakaari and broke the surface again and again on the way south — at Rotorua, Tarawera, the boiling ground around Taupō, and at last Tongariro. Every place they surfaced is a real geothermal or volcanic centre, and in sequence they trace, almost exactly, the line of the Taupō Volcanic Zone. The tradition describes a single vein of underground fire running from the sea to the mountains — which is precisely what the rift is.

There is a name for traditions that carry a real geological event down the generations — geomythology — and their reach can be startling: across the Tasman, Aboriginal Australian stories appear to preserve the shape of coastlines the sea drowned more than seven thousand years ago. Ngātoroirangi's fire belongs to that company — a memory that holds the form of a rift no one could ever have seen whole.

Chapter Five

The newcomers read the weather,
and clear the forest

Europeans arrived expecting a Britain of the South. They found a wetter, windier, more violent country — and set about remaking its surface.

To the British settlers of the nineteenth century, New Zealand's climate was a selling point: temperate, green, familiar enough to grow English grass and graze English sheep. The reality was less obliging — a narrow, mountainous country in the path of the Roaring Forties, where the weather changes by the hour and the rivers flood without warning.

Their largest mark on the climate was indirect. To make pasture, they burned and felled the forest at a pace that dwarfed what had come before. Forest that had covered most of the country before people arrived — already reduced by earlier Māori burning — was cut back to less than a third of the land.

It was the first deliberate, mass reshaping of the New Zealand environment by its newest arrivals. It would not be the last — and the next would be invisible.

Chapter Six

Tarawera, 1886,
and the night the terraces died

The country's first great tourist wonder, the Pink and White Terraces, vanished in a single night — and the land gave warning first.

In the early hours of 10 June 1886, Mount Tarawera split open along a rift many kilometres long and erupted. Ash buried the surrounding villages; about 120 people died, most of them Māori. The Pink and White Terraces — Te Otukapuarangi and Te Tarata, silica staircases that drew visitors from across the world — were destroyed. They were perhaps the largest deposits of silica sinter — geyserite — anywhere on Earth, built up over centuries from the mineral-laden water of the geothermal springs and geysers below.

Eleven days before, a boatload of tourists crossing the lake to the terraces saw a war canoe — a waka taua, crowded with figures — glide through the mist and vanish. It matched no canoe known on the water. An aged Māori priest, the tohunga Tūhoto Ariki, read it as a warning: the people would be punished for selling the sacred terraces to sightseers. Whether it was a mirage, or a swell thrown up by the mountain already stirring, has been argued ever since — but the destruction it foretold was not in doubt. When Tarawera erupted, Tūhoto — reputedly over a hundred years old — was buried in his house and dug out alive four days later.

A wonder that had taken centuries to deposit was gone in hours. The land that built it took it back.

Chapter Seven

The settler century shakes,
and rebuilds in Art Deco

Twice the colonial era was reshaped by the ground itself — once raising a capital out of the sea, once flattening a city and rebuilding it whole.

In 1855, a magnitude-8.2 earthquake on the Wairarapa fault — the largest in New Zealand's recorded history — heaved the land around Wellington upward by metres. Much of the modern central city stands on ground that, before that night, was under the harbour.

On 3 February 1931, a magnitude-7.8 earthquake struck Hawke's Bay. It killed 256 people — the country's deadliest natural disaster — and razed Napier and Hastings. The seabed off Napier rose by around two metres, draining a lagoon and adding land the city still uses. Napier rebuilt fast, in the clean lines of the moment, and became one of the world's great Art Deco towns: a disaster legible in an architectural style.

Act III · The Changed Air
An invisible reshaping
c. 1850 — 2000
Chapter Eight

The Anthropocene arrives,
and the air starts to change

For most of human history the air held about 280 parts of carbon dioxide per million. In a century and a half, we have pushed it past 420 — and New Zealand has measured the change from its own clean corner of the planet.

Bubbles of ancient air, trapped in Antarctic ice, show carbon dioxide holding remarkably steady for thousands of years — around 280 parts per million through the whole rise of human civilisation.

280 ppm
The pre-industrial baseline

In 1958, a young scientist named Charles Keeling began measuring CO₂ on a Hawaiian volcano. His record — the Keeling Curve — caught the air in the act of changing, climbing year after year.

In 1972, New Zealand opened its own station at Baring Head, on the wild coast south of Wellington, sampling air that has crossed the Southern Ocean. It is the longest continuous CO₂ record in the Southern Hemisphere.

1972
Baring Head record begins, at 326 ppm

Both records now read past 420 parts per million — a level the planet has not seen in millions of years.

The same air sits over the whole Earth. Carbon was not the only thing we put into it — another gas, rising from the world's refrigerators and spray cans, would by the 1980s open a wound in the sky directly above the South.

The country has noticed. In 2009, when the Baring Head record was already four decades long, about — of New Zealanders agreed that climate change is real. The country that built the longest carbon record in the south now sits among the world's most worried about what the record is showing.

Chapter Nine

The hole in the sky,
and why the south paid most

A class of miracle chemicals tore a hole in the ozone layer over Antarctica. Of all the inhabited world, New Zealand and Australia sat closest to the damage.

In 1974, two chemists warned that chlorofluorocarbons — CFCs, the inert gases prized in fridges, aerosols and foam — would drift to the stratosphere and destroy ozone. In 1985, British scientists confirmed it in the worst possible way: each austral spring, a hole was opening in the ozone layer over Antarctica.

When measurements began, the hole was small. Through the 1980s it opened wider every spring as chlorine from CFCs built up in the stratosphere over the cold Antarctic vortex.

In 1987, the world signed the Montreal Protocol and began phasing out CFCs — the most effective environmental treaty ever made. But the chemicals already aloft would keep destroying ozone for decades.

1987
Montreal Protocol signed

The hole kept growing into the 2000s, peaking at nearly 30 million square kilometres — larger than the whole of North America, and far larger than Antarctica itself. New Zealand and Australia sit just north of its edge: the hole rarely covers them, but ozone-poor air spills north over the Southern Ocean toward them each spring.

29.9M km²
Record maximum, year 2000

Since the mid-2000s the hole has stopped growing and slowly begun to heal. Full recovery over Antarctica is projected for around 2066 — within a lifetime of the warning.

It is a rare climate success story: the countries of the world noticed a problem and coordinated to solve it.

New Zealand already had naturally thin ozone and exceptionally clear air; peak summer UV runs around forty per cent higher than at comparable latitudes in the north. The country has, with Australia, the highest rates of melanoma in the world. The ozone hole did not create that risk on its own — but it sharpened it, over exactly the people living beneath it.

The replacements, HFCs, spare the ozone and warm the planet instead. The treaty that closed the hole handed the problem back to the climate.

Chapter Ten

Still shaking,
and learning to live with it

The deep geology never went quiet. In the last fifteen years it has destroyed a city's heart and rewritten a coastline.

On 4 September 2010, a magnitude-7.1 earthquake struck near Christchurch in the early morning. Remarkably, no one died. Then, on 22 February 2011, a shallow magnitude-6.3 aftershock hit directly beneath the city at lunchtime. It killed 185 people, collapsed buildings, and turned whole suburbs to liquefied silt.

The lesson was written into the country. Magnitude alone means little; depth and location mean everything. The shallow 6.3 beneath Christchurch was deadlier than the larger Kaikōura quake of 2016, which ruptured more than twenty faults at once — a world record — yet killed two. New Zealand now builds, insures and plans for a ground that will move again.

The memory of those mornings lives in the people who were there. The gap has narrowed a little since 2011 but never closed; in every year on record Canterbury reports earthquake worry at two to three times the national rate, with smaller spikes around the 2016 Kaikōura rupture and the 2020 Kermadec swarm. Chapter Eleven

The heat arrives,
and the summers lengthen

The country has warmed by about 1.1°C in a century. The change is most visible in the summers — on land, and in an ocean that has begun to run a fever of its own.

New Zealand's longest temperature record, the seven-station series, shows the country roughly 1.1°C warmer than it was in 1909. The reanalysis record below tells the same story a different way — and the warmest years in it have clustered in the last decade.

The warming is not only on land. In the summer of 2017–18, a marine heatwave settled over the Tasman Sea, with surface waters up to several degrees above normal; it was the country's hottest summer on record. Such marine heatwaves are becoming more frequent and more intense, reshaping fisheries and coastlines.

Heat is the hazard that does not announce itself with a bang. It arrives as longer summers, warmer nights, and a sea that no longer cools the way it did.

How worried the country is about that arrival depends a great deal on where the country is standing. Chapter Twelve

The climates ahead,
and the choice that shapes them

How much hotter New Zealand becomes is not yet decided. The projections fan out from the present according to how much carbon the world keeps adding.

The latest projections show the country's path splitting on the choices of this century. Under deep cuts, New Zealand settles around a degree above today's climate. Under high emissions, it keeps climbing — past three degrees by the 2090s, with longer droughts in the north and east, heavier downpours, and a rising sea.

Sea-level rise is already locked in to a degree: planners are told to prepare for around 0.8 metres by the 2090s, more beyond. For a country whose cities cluster on the coast — much of it, like central Wellington and Napier, on ground the earth itself only recently delivered — the ocean's slow return is the quiet counterpart to the sudden violence of a quake.

The country is split on how clean and green the archipelago still is. Across the whole adult population the quality of New Zealand's natural environment rates around — out of seven. The country that has changed the most is the country least satisfied with what is left.

The fire and the water made this land. The air is now in flux.

Chapter Thirteen

The made land,
still being made

A country assembled by forces beyond any human hand is, for the first time, being reshaped by one.

Aotearoa is the visible fraction of a drowned continent, raised on a plate boundary, studded with volcanoes, shaken on a schedule no one can quite predict. Fire and water built it, and still do.

For all of its settled history, the land made the people: where they could live, what they could grow, when they had to run. The newest chapter reverses the sentence. The forest fell to the axe; the air thinned under our chemicals; the climate is bending to our exhaust. The geology will outlast us. The weather, now, is partly ours.

Geology made the archipelago, and continues to make it — but now a changing climate shapes it too.
